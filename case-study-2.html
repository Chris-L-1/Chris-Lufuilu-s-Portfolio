Project Overview

Fika is an original product concept — a child-only student transport service for Cape Town parents of primary and high school learners. The name comes from Swahili, meaning to arrive. Swahili is my home language. The name was chosen deliberately. The entire product exists to answer one question a parent asks every school morning: did my child arrive?

The service operates a vetted fleet of company-owned vehicles running fixed routes across Cape Town. Parents register, add their children, select their school area, and get assigned to a driver. All drivers are employed by the company, background-checked, and driving-assessed before their first route.

This is a dual-interface product — one app for parents, one for drivers. Both serve the same core purpose from opposite sides: getting children to school and home safely, with full visibility and accountability at every step.

UX Stages

Two Users. One Problem.

Most apps serve one user. Fika serves two — with different needs, different fears, and different definitions of safety. Both are served by the same data.

User 1 — Parent

Visibility

A working parent who cannot be present when their child travels to school. They need to know the child arrived safely without relying on the child to remember to call.

User 2 — Driver

Protection

A vetted driver responsible for other people's children. They need verifiable records of every pickup and drop-off so they cannot be falsely accused of something they did not do.

Core Product Insight

The parent has no visibility. The driver has no proof. The same feature — a timestamped confirmation — solves both problems simultaneously.

Stage 1

Empathy and Psychology

The Core Emotional Insight

Before research, wireframes, or features — the emotional foundation of Fika was identified through empathy work.

When a parent puts their child in the Fika car for the very first time and the car drives away, they do not feel relief. They do not feel trust. What they feel is controlled anxiety. The car is gone and their child is in it.

This means Fika's design challenge is not registration or payment. The real challenge is: how does Fika convert first-time anxiety into earned trust, one trip at a time?

Cognitive Biases in Play

01

Familiarity Bias

Parents know Uber and Bolt. They arrive at Fika expecting the same mental model — map, moving dot, ETA. Deviating from that pattern without reason destroys trust immediately.

The tracking screen must feel instantly familiar — not copied, but recognisable.
02

Paradox of Choice

A parent registering is already anxious. Too many questions at once — children, schools, areas, payment — and they abandon before completing.

Onboarding must be broken into small committed steps. One decision at a time.
03

Anchoring Bias

If price appears before trust is established, it feels expensive. After seeing vetting, tracking, and accountability — the same number feels fair.

Price is revealed late in onboarding, after value is demonstrated.
04

Loss Aversion

Parents are not paying for convenience. They are paying to avoid loss — specifically the loss of their child's safety.

Fika's messaging focuses on what parents no longer have to worry about.
05

Von Restorff Effect

On the driver's manifest, if all children look identical nothing stands out. A child with a medical note or gate code must be visually distinct.

Critical child information must be visually distinct on the driver's screen.
06

Peak End Rule

Parents judge the whole experience on two moments — when the car drives away the first time, and when the arrived notification lands.

The arrived notification is not a system message. It is the moment a parent exhales.

Problem Statement

Cape Town parents of primary and high school learners struggle to trust their children's safety travelling to and from school — because there is no affordable, child-only, trustworthy transport solution that gives them real visibility into whether their child arrived safely.

Stage 2

Parent Research Findings

  • 01
    Geographic word-of-mouth limitationTrusted driver recommendations only work within a neighbourhood. A good driver in Parow cannot help a parent in Bellville.
  • 02
    Informal = untrustworthyA neighbour offering transport is not the same as a company offering transport. The difference is accountability.
  • 03
    Price is non-negotiableAffordable does not mean cheap — it means fair for what you earn. Single income households need a price that makes sense.
  • 04
    Parents want institutional accountabilityA name, a face, a number, something registered. Verbal agreements offer no protection if something goes wrong.
  • 05
    Reliability is non-negotiableEvery morning. Not maybe. The informal system breaks when a driver decides to stop — sometimes with no notice at all.
  • 06
    Legal accountability mattersA registered company signals seriousness. If something happens to a child, the parent needs someone they can hold responsible.
  • 07
    One notification changes everythingA single message saying the child arrived safely would be emotionally transformative for a working parent sitting at a desk with their phone off.
  • 08
    Fika sells relief, not transportThe core emotional value of the product is the feeling of being a good parent despite impossible circumstances.

Sipho plans his route the night before — there is no time to think in the morning. His biggest daily stress is not the driving. It is uncertainty: children not ready, parents who knew last night that their child would not attend but said nothing.

He described an incident involving a child named Kavelo. Sipho dropped him at school and watched him walk through the gate. That afternoon the mother called — the school said he was never in class. She accused Sipho of not taking him. For three hours he had nothing to show her. No timestamp. No record. Kavelo had left through a hole in the fence during break. The mother apologised — but the three hours without proof stayed with Sipho long after.

"I just want to do it properly. With the right tools. So that everyone — the parents, the children, me — everyone feels safe and respected. That is all I want."

— Sipho M., research participant

Driver Research Findings

  • 01
    Drivers absorb financial losses from silenceEvery unnecessary trip costs fuel from the driver's own pocket. No formal system requires parents to notify absences in advance.
  • 02
    Favour vs professional serviceParents treat informal transport as a favour. A company structure changes that dynamic completely.
  • 03
    Children exploit accountability gapsWhen children know there is no tracking system they learn to exploit it. Confirmation systems close that loophole.
  • 04
    Drivers want to be trackedNot because they are forced to — because it protects them. The same data that reassures a parent clears a driver of a false accusation.
  • 05
    The interface must be fast and minimalEvery extra decision the app requires during driving is a direct safety risk to the children in the car.
  • 06
    Route data enables backup driversIf a driver is unavailable, a replacement can run the route without starting from zero. This emerged purely from research.
  • 07
    The system is the witnessTimestamped confirmations transform the app from a convenience tool into a legal protection layer.
  • 08
    GPS should trigger confirmation automaticallyThe app detects school location and surfaces only the children assigned there. The driver should never have to search.
  • 09
    The app handles communication — the driver handles drivingAutomated notifications remove the communication burden entirely from the driver.
  • 10
    Cognitive load must be near zeroThree seconds maximum screen interaction per stop. More than that is a safety risk, not just a UX problem.
  • 11
    Driver profile visibility builds trust before arrivalName, photo, and car registration visible to the parent before the car arrives. Transforms a stranger into a known person.
  • 12
    Trip ratings build official reputationGood drivers deserve a formal record beyond word of mouth. Ratings protect quality drivers and surface problematic ones.
  • 13
    Incident reporting protects everyoneA formal channel for documenting in-car safety issues. Currently drivers report verbally — with no record either way.
Cross-Interview Insight

Both participants — without knowing each other existed — described the same gap from opposite sides. The parent has no visibility. The driver has no proof. The same timestamped confirmation solves both problems simultaneously. That is the core product insight of Fika confirmed by independent research.

Synthesis — Clusters

01
Trust and Accountability
Parent Findings 2, 4, 6 · Driver Findings 2, 4, 7, 11

Parents do not trust individuals without formal structure. Drivers want to be tracked because it protects them. Legal accountability matters to both sides.

Pattern: Trust is not assumed. It is built through structure, visibility, and formal accountability on both sides.
02
Communication Breakdown
Parent Findings 1, 5, 7 · Driver Findings 1, 9

Every pain point traces back to a communication gap. Word of mouth has geographic limits. Reliability breaks when communication is informal.

Pattern: The app's primary job is to close the communication gap between parent and driver automatically.
03
Safety and Protection
Parent Findings 3, 6, 8 · Driver Findings 3, 7, 12, 13

Safety means something different to each user. For parents it is emotional. For drivers it is legal.

Pattern: The app must serve both definitions of safety simultaneously with every interaction.
04
Operational Efficiency
Driver Findings 5, 6, 8, 9, 10

The driver interface is a safety-critical tool. Three seconds maximum interaction. GPS automation. Every second of unnecessary interaction is a risk.

Pattern: Efficiency is not a convenience feature — it is a child protection feature.
05
Emotional Value
Parent Findings 7, 8 · Driver Findings 4, 11

Both users need to feel something from this app — not just use it. One notification transforms a parent's morning. Profile visibility humanises a driver before arrival.

Pattern: The emotional experience is the product.

Insights

01

Trust is infrastructure

Parents and drivers both arrive at Fika already suspicious of each other. The app cannot assume goodwill. It must build trust actively through visible structure, vetted profiles, and formal accountability before the first trip happens.

02

The app is the communication layer

Every breakdown in the current informal system happens because communication depends on humans remembering. The app must remove human memory from the equation entirely.

03

Safety has two currencies

For parents safety is emotional. For drivers safety is legal. Fika must pay both currencies with every interaction. A confirmation tap is not just a notification — it is a legal timestamp.

04

The driver interface is a safety-critical tool

A driver distracted by a complicated interface has children in the car. Three seconds maximum is not a design preference — it is a child protection requirement.

05

Fika sells relief, not transport

Nobody opens Fika because they love the idea of a school transport app. They open it because they are afraid. Every screen must be measured against whether it moves the parent from afraid to relieved.

How Might We Questions

From Insight 1 — Trust is infrastructure
Surface

How might we show a parent everything they need to trust their driver before the first pickup arrives?

Structural

How might we design an onboarding experience that builds institutional trust before a parent ever meets their driver in person?

Deep

How might we create a product that makes a parent feel safer trusting a vetted company than trusting a neighbour they have known for years?

From Insight 2 — The app is the communication layer
Surface

How might we ensure a parent receives an arrival notification without the driver having to remember to send one?

Structural

How might we design an absence notification system that makes it easier for a parent to notify than to forget?

Deep

How might we remove human memory entirely from the communication chain between parent and driver?

From Insight 3 — Safety has two currencies
Surface

How might we design a confirmation tap that simultaneously reassures the parent and protects the driver?

Structural

How might we build a record system that a driver can reference as legal evidence if an accusation is made?

Deep

How might we design an app where every single interaction serves both the parent's emotional need and the driver's legal need at the same time?

From Insight 4 — The driver interface is a safety-critical tool
Surface

How might we design a confirmation screen that a driver can complete in under three seconds without taking their eyes off the road?

Structural

How might we use GPS to do the organising so the driver only has to confirm — never to navigate the app?

Deep

How might we design a driver interface where the app thinks ahead so the driver never has to?

From Insight 5 — Fika sells relief, not transport
Surface

How might we design an arrival notification that feels warm and human rather than like a system message?

Structural

How might we design the tracking screen so that watching the car move converts anxiety into calm in real time?

Deep

How might we design an entire product experience that makes a parent feel like a good parent every single school morning — regardless of whether they can be there in person?

The Moment That Matters

Nomsa is at her desk. Phone is off. It is 7:45. Amahle is 9 and walked to school with a neighbour's child. Liam is 14 — small for his age — and took the minibus taxi alone past the men who gather near the rank every morning. Nomsa will not know if either of them arrived safely until her lunch break. That feeling sits with her every single school morning. Fika exists to end that moment.

Key Design Insight

Nomsa is not paying for transport. She is paying to feel like a good mother despite impossible circumstances. The arrived notification is not a system message — it is the moment she exhales. It must feel warm and immediate. Price must only appear after she has seen vetting, tracking, and accountability. Shown too early it feels expensive. Shown after trust is built, it feels fair.
