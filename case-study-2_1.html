Project Overview

Fika is an original product concept — a child-only student transport service built for Cape Town parents of primary and high school learners. The name comes from Swahili, meaning to arrive. Swahili is my home language. The name was chosen deliberately. The entire product exists to answer one question a parent asks every school morning: did my child arrive?

The service operates a vetted fleet of company-owned vehicles on fixed routes across Cape Town. All drivers are employed, background-checked, and driving-assessed. Parents register, select their school area, and get assigned to a driver. Monthly subscription. No guesswork. No word of mouth.

The Problem

Cape Town parents of primary and high school learners struggle to trust their children's safety travelling to and from school — because there is no affordable, child-only, trustworthy transport solution that gives them real visibility into whether their child arrived safely.

Parents currently solve this through word of mouth — a neighbour recommends a driver, a friend vouches for someone. That system has no formal accountability and breaks the moment the informal driver decides to stop.

When a parent puts their child in a car for the first time and it drives away, they do not feel relief. They feel controlled anxiety. Fika's real design challenge is: how do you convert first-time anxiety into earned trust, one trip at a time?

Two Users. One Problem.

Fika is a dual-interface product. Different needs, different fears, different definitions of safety — all served by the same data.

User 1 — Parent

Visibility

A working parent who cannot be present when their child travels to school. They need to know the child arrived safely without relying on the child to remember to call.

User 2 — Driver

Protection

A vetted driver responsible for other people's children. They need verifiable records of every pickup and drop-off so they cannot be falsely accused of something they did not do.

Core Product Insight — Confirmed by Independent Research

The parent has no visibility. The driver has no proof. The same timestamped confirmation solves both problems simultaneously. One feature. Two users. Multiple problems resolved.

Key Insights

Five insights distilled from 21 research findings across two user interviews. These drive every design decision that follows.

01

Trust is infrastructure

Parents and drivers both arrive at Fika already suspicious of each other. The app must build trust actively through visible structure, vetted profiles, and formal accountability before the first trip happens.

02

The app is the communication layer

Every breakdown in the informal system happens because communication depends on humans remembering. The app must remove human memory from the equation entirely — notifications and confirmations happen automatically.

03

Safety has two currencies

For parents safety is emotional. For drivers safety is legal. A confirmation tap is not just a notification — it is a legal timestamp. Every interaction must serve both definitions simultaneously.

04

The driver interface is a safety-critical tool

A driver distracted by a complicated screen has children in the car. Three seconds maximum interaction per stop is not a design preference — it is a child protection requirement.

05

Fika sells relief, not transport

Nobody opens Fika because they love a transport app. They open it because they are afraid. Every screen must move the parent from afraid to relieved. The arrived notification is the moment a parent exhales.

The Moment That Matters

Nomsa is at her desk. Phone is off. It is 7:45. Amahle is 9 and walked with a neighbour's child. Liam is 14 and took the minibus taxi alone past the men near the rank. Nomsa will not know if either of them arrived safely until her lunch break. That feeling sits with her every single school morning. Fika exists to end that moment.

Key Design Insight

Nomsa is not paying for transport. She is paying to feel like a good mother despite impossible circumstances. The arrived notification is the moment she exhales. It must feel warm and immediate. Price must only appear after she has seen vetting, tracking, and accountability — shown too early it feels expensive, shown after trust is built it feels fair.

Sipho dropped Kavelo at school and watched him walk through the gate. That afternoon the mother called — the school said he was never in class. She accused Sipho of not bringing him. For three hours, Sipho had nothing to show her. No timestamp. No record. Kavelo had left through a hole in the fence. The mother apologised — but those three hours without proof stayed with Sipho long after. This is not hypothetical. This already happened.

Key Design Insight

Sipho does not resist being tracked — he wants it. The same data that reassures a parent is the same data that clears him of a false accusation. Every interaction on his interface must be completable in under 3 seconds. Children are in the car. Anything longer is a safety risk, not just a UX problem.

Stage 3 — Problem Framing & Information Architecture

Research findings needed a structure to live in. This stage refined the problem statement and translated 21 findings into two clear information architectures — one per interface — built entirely around what each user actually needs, not a generic app template.

Cape Town parents of primary and high school learners struggle to trust their children's safety travelling to and from school — because there is no affordable, child-only, trustworthy transport solution that gives them real visibility into whether their child arrived safely. The current system offers no safety assurance for drivers, no protection when parents act out of fear, and no structured communication channel when something unexpected happens. It also creates a road safety risk when drivers navigate unfamiliar routes with children in the car.

— Refined Problem Statement, Stage 3

The Core IA Insight

The parent's tracking screen and the driver's active route screen are powered by the same live data. The parent sees the car moving toward the school. The driver generates that movement. One data source. Two interfaces. Two users served simultaneously.

What's Next

Two stages remain, and they're sequenced deliberately rather than skipped.

○ Stage 5 — Usability Testing

Not yet run — and here's why

Testing a partial interface risks surfacing problems that a completed information architecture would have already resolved on its own. All 25 screens across both interfaces were only just finished, so priority went into completing full coverage first — testing comes next, against a whole product rather than fragments of one.

The test plan itself was written back in Stage 1, before a single screen existed:

  • Registration: Can a first-time parent complete registration without confusion or abandonment?
  • Tracking: Does the parent feel reassured the moment they see the tracking screen?
  • Driver route: Can a driver complete pickups, navigation, and confirmations without looking at the screen for more than 3 seconds at a time?
  • Arrival: Does the arrived notification land as warm and trustworthy, or cold and robotic?
○ Stage 6 — Strategic Leadership Thinking

Following testing, not before it

This final stage connects Fika's design decisions to business outcomes — the monetisation model, what actually drives retention, and how a trust-building product like this creates defensible value for a transport company rather than just a nice user experience. It's sequenced after usability testing on purpose: real user feedback should inform which business risks get prioritised first, not the other way around.
